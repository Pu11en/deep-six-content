A fire ant colony doesn’t always have a mound you can see. In a drought, the ground itself can be too hard and dry for them to build one.

A colony has more going on than workers walking around outside. It also has a developing generation underground: eggs, larvae and pupae. Together, those stages are called brood. Eggs hatch into larvae, larvae become pupae, and adults eventually emerge.

Those young ants are part of the reason nest conditions matter. During cold, wet weather, colonies tend to occupy higher parts of the nest, where they can take advantage of sunlight and keep brood away from waterlogged soil. During hot, dry weather, cooler, moister conditions below ground become useful instead.

Workers move the brood within the nest as conditions change. Picture the nursery shifting between different parts of the structure, rather than every ant staying in one fixed room all season.

So the nest you see above ground doesn’t show you where the developing ants are being kept. The colony’s internal activity can continue out of sight while the surface looks quiet.

For a homeowner, that changes what a missing mound means. You’re losing a visible clue about the nest, not getting a direct measurement of the colony’s condition or its developing young.
