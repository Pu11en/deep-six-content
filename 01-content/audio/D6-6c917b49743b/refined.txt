A fire ant colony doesn’t always have a mound you can see. In a drought, the ground itself can be too hard and dry for them to build one.

But here’s the part you don’t see. Underground, workers are caring for the colony’s next generation. Eggs, larvae, and pupae. Together, those developing stages are called brood. Eggs hatch into larvae. Larvae become pupae. And eventually, adult ants emerge.

Where those young ants stay depends on conditions inside the nest.

In cold, wet weather, higher parts of the nest can provide warmth from sunlight and keep the brood away from waterlogged soil. In hot, dry weather, the cooler, moister spaces below ground become more useful.

So the workers move the brood. Picture a nursery shifting between different parts of the nest as conditions change. The developing ants don’t stay in one fixed room all season.

That’s why a quiet-looking lawn can be misleading. You’re seeing the surface. You’re not seeing where the workers are keeping the young, or what’s happening in the chambers below.

A missing mound takes away one of your clues. It doesn’t show you whether the colony is gone. The part you can see above ground is only part of the story. The nursery can be farther below your feet.
