# Deep Six spoken narration

Write a clear, interesting explanation that a homeowner understands on the first listen.

## Build one connected lesson

- Keep the selected hook exactly as the first paragraph. Pay off its question in the first short paragraph after it. A clear action sequence can supply the answer; do not give a broad answer and then repeat it in longer wording.
- Choose one useful lesson. Develop the insect story far enough to explain it. Facts are available material, not a checklist to include.
- Decide the lesson's sequence before drafting. Select the few facts that serve it. A recognition lesson does not also need a complete life-cycle lesson and a trap-placement lesson.
- Give the viewer something concrete to recognize, understand or do. Recognizing a harmless insect can be the practical lesson. Do not bolt unrelated household tips onto an interesting story.
- When Drew likes a story and asks for a better ending, preserve the working story. Add the missing connection to a homeowner's decision, inspection or prevention step. Let that useful thought finish before the brand sign-off.
- Each sentence must add understanding. Cut repeated answers, obvious consequences, announcements of importance and generic summaries.
- Give the detail directly. Cut sentences such as "You can recognize it by its size and the way it moves" when the next sentence already describes those features.
- Let the lesson set the length. No word target and no padding to match an earlier episode.

## Make it sound spoken

- Talk to one homeowner. Use contractions, familiar words and connected thoughts with varied sentence lengths.
- Introduce an unfamiliar insect plainly. Explain a useful technical term where it appears, or use ordinary words instead.
- Connect a name to the visible feature that explains it. Avoid a separate dictionary-definition sentence.
- Keep subjects and sequences clear. If a young insect becomes an adult, explain that change before describing what the adult does.
- Use concrete descriptions. Say sticky traps, with a brief explanation of the glue surface if needed. Say cardboard boxes.
- Replace technical labels with specific everyday descriptions. Vague substitutes such as "body part" or "egg-laying structure" still leave the listener guessing; describe the thin tube used to lay eggs.
- Avoid brochure language, research-report language, choppy fact lists and padded moral-of-the-story endings.
- Never use em dashes.

## Keep evidence and branding in their proper places

- Use only the supplied checked facts and respect their species, location and certainty limits. A sighting is not proof of an infestation. A beneficial insect is not a promise of complete pest control.
- Keep claims narrow enough to stay accurate. Do not recite every source limitation or end on an unnecessary disclaimer when the script never made the disputed claim.
- Keep university names, source credits, citations, links and production directions outside the spoken script. Preserve fact IDs in the internal fields.
- Finish the teaching thought. Then put the approved sign-off, exactly once, in its own final paragraph. A natural breath is enough; no forced slogan bridge or extra call to action.
- The same episode narrator reads the lesson and sign-off. Do not write editing cues into the narration.

## Edit before Drew sees it

A separate editing call receives the draft, this brief and the same checked facts.

1. Follow the words as a first-time listener. Find vague subjects, missing steps, stiff phrasing and unexplained terms.
2. Remove any sentence that repeats a point, states an obvious consequence or wanders into a second lesson. Check that the remaining sequence still makes sense.
3. Check every factual claim against its supplied evidence and limits. Fix or remove unsupported claims. Never invent a fact to repair a transition.
4. Check that the hook pays off, the lesson has practical meaning and the final thought leads comfortably into the exact sign-off.
5. Return the edited script and concise change notes. Report unresolved problems honestly. One editing pass is allowed; unresolved problems stop automatic presentation.

Passing these checks means ready for Drew's review. It does not mean Drew approved the script or that audience performance is proven.
